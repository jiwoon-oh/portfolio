Case study · Experimental design & causal inference

When the data won't pick a winner

A restaurant chain tested three promotions for a new menu item across 137 locations. After four weeks the data refused to crown a winner — but it named a loser with confidence, and put a price tag on keeping it.

01

Design

What was asked, and what the analysis tried to disprove

Because promotions were randomly assigned at the store level, this is a real experiment — causal language is earned here, not borrowed.

Each of 137 locations ran a single promotion for four weeks.

Null hypothesis H₀

Mean sales are equal across the three promotions.
μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃

Alternative H₁

At least one promotion differs in mean sales.

Alpha

0.05

Main test

One-way ANOVA

Post hoc

Tukey HSD

Three categorical levels, a continuous outcome, a comparison of means — ANOVA fits. Its F test is non-directional, so it can say that the groups differ but never which one wins. That belongs to the post hoc stage.

03

Unit of analysis

Feed it 548 rows and the p-value will lie to you

Each store appears as four weekly rows, so the raw file offers 548 observations where the design only earns 137.

A store's week 1 and week 2 are not independent. Feeding all 548 rows to ANOVA inflates the sample fourfold and shrinks the p-value accordingly. Since assignment was per store, each location was collapsed to its four-week mean. That discards information, so the decision needed evidence:

Week carries no signal

F = 0.028 · p = 0.9935

Week 1 and week 4 are indistinguishable. Averaging destroys no trend because there is no trend.

Variance lives between stores

Between 16.19 · Within 4.73

Store-to-store spread is roughly 3.4× the week-to-week spread. Weekly wobble is noise, not signal.

The alternative wasn't worth it

Repeated measures / mixed model

A mixed model would preserve weekly structure — but the structure it preserves is noise, so the added complexity buys nothing.

04

Assumptions

Normality failed; equal variance held

Normality was rejected in all three groups — so the result had to survive a test that doesn't assume it.

Shapiro-Wilk normality rejected

P1 p = .0002 · P2 p < .0001 · P3 p = .0001

Driven by a handful of high-revenue stores in one market, not a fundamentally different distribution.

Levene equal variance met

stat = 0.468 · p = 0.6272

Group variances are statistically indistinguishable.

ANOVA alone would have been hard to defend; a rank-based test alone would have cost the ability to state the gap in dollars. With balanced groups and equal variances, the compromise was to run both and require them to agree.

05

Main test

There is a difference — but it isn't the whole story

Both tests reject the null, and both land in the same place.

ANOVA F

5.846

p-value

0.0037

Effect size η²

0.080

Kruskal-Wallis

p = 0.0002

Agreement between the parametric and rank-based tests means the normality violation is not driving the result.

η² = 0.080 means promotion explains about 8% of the variance in store sales. The other 92% belongs elsewhere — market size, mostly. Significant and important are different claims: this effect is real without being dominant, and saying so is more useful than overselling it.

06

Post hoc

Which pair, and by how much

Promotion 2 loses to both alternatives. Promotions 1 and 3 cannot be told apart.

P1 · P2 Promotion 2 earns $10,770 less per store-week than Promotion 1 (−18.5%) p = .004 · d = 0.71 significant
P1 · P3 Promotion 3 trails Promotion 1 by $2,734, but the interval contains zero p = .686 · d = 0.17 no difference
P2 · P3 Promotion 3 earns $8,035 more per store-week than Promotion 2 (+17.0%) p = .037 · d = 0.52 significant

The ordering is Promotion 1 ≈ Promotion 3 > Promotion 2. The experiment answered what to drop, not what to pick.

07

Robustness

Telling a bad reading apart from a real one

Of 46 flagged outliers, 45 came from one large market — structure, not error.

Those stores sell more because the market is bigger. Deleting them would delete signal rather than clean noise.

No digit-shift trace suggests a transcription error, and no column records closures or stockouts. With no grounds to delete it the value stayed — and the analysis checked whether it mattered instead.

Original

F = 5.846 · p = 0.0037

Store 507, week 2 removed

F = 5.778 · p = 0.0039

The conclusion does not rest on a single observation, so all 548 rows were kept.

Reporting the check is the point. An outlier decision without a sensitivity test is an opinion.

08

Confounders

Was Promotion 2 simply dealt a worse hand?

If Promotion 2 had been handed the smaller markets, the whole finding would collapse. It wasn't.

Promotion × market size independent

χ² = 1.189 · p = 0.880

No promotion clustered into larger or smaller markets.

Promotion × store age no difference

F = 0.450 · p = 0.639

Store tenure is balanced across the three groups.

Two-way ANOVA controlling for market size

Promotion

F = 16.04

Market size

F = 96.13

Interaction

p = 0.266

Adding market size pushed the promotion F statistic up from 5.85 to 16.04. A confounder would have weakened the effect once controlled; instead it sharpened. Market size drives sales hard but is unrelated to assignment, so it was acting as noise — and removing that noise exposed the promotion effect. The interaction is not significant, so the effect does not change with market size.

09

Segments

Does it hold everywhere — and what does repeating the test cost?

Splitting into segments means running three tests — which is exactly how false positives get manufactured.

At α = .05, three tests return at least one significant result about 14% of the time even when nothing is there. Bonferroni correction was applied before reading anything into them.

SegmentnRaw pBonferroniVerdict
Small150.00320.0096significant
Medium800.00010.0003significant
Large420.00770.0232significant

All three survive correction, and Benjamini-Hochberg agrees. Promotion 2's weakness is a consistent pattern, not one lucky segment. The caveat: small markets hold only 15 stores, roughly five per arm, so that row carries less weight than the other two.

How to read these numbers

The gap is $10,770 per store-week — modest until it compounds across 47 stores and four weeks. Stated the other way: $2M is not what one restaurant lost. Without naming the scale, the figure reads as inflation.

It is also a point estimate. Propagating the Tukey interval puts the total between $0.55M and $3.5M. Two million is the most plausible value in that range, not a settled amount — and since the outcome is revenue, the cost of running each promotion is not in these figures.

11

Recommendation

What the data settled, and what it left open

Drop one, and admit the other two are tied.

Drop  Do not roll out Promotion 2

It clears every check — significance (p = .004), a medium effect (d = 0.71), no confounding, consistency across all three segments, and stability when the one questionable observation is removed. The recommendation does not hang on any single test.

Tied  Revenue cannot separate Promotions 1 and 3

Tukey found no significant gap (p = .686), and splitting by market size flipped the direction rather than clarifying it (Small −0.65 · Medium −2.20 · Large +1.97, in $ thousands).

The choice has to be made outside this dataset — on execution cost, operational complexity, and inventory load. Naming what is missing is more honest than forcing a winner out of a tie, and it tells the business what to measure next.

12

Limitations

What this analysis cannot answer

Four weeks of revenue data cannot answer everything the decision needs.

A four-week window

Nothing here distinguishes a durable effect from a novelty effect. That requires sales beyond week four.

Information lost to aggregation

Collapsing to store-level means bought independence at the cost of any week-to-week pattern.

No cost data

The comparison is on revenue. If the promotions differ in cost to run, the margin ranking could differ from this one.

A thin segment

Small markets contribute about five stores per arm. The result survived correction but warrants re-testing with more data.

What to collect next

Execution cost per promotion (labor, marketing spend, inventory load)  ·  sales beyond week four  ·  additional small-market locations
