01

Overview

What the company was trying to decide

A chain about to launch a new menu item had three candidate promotions and no basis for choosing between them.

Each of 137 stores was assigned a single promotion and its weekly sales were recorded for four weeks. Because assignment happened at the store level and was randomized, the data supports causal claims rather than correlational ones.

This project evaluates whether the three strategies produced genuinely different sales performance, and converts that answer into a recommendation the business can act on.

02

Business Question

The decision the analysis has to serve

Which of the three promotional strategies generated the strongest sales performance?

  • Did average sales differ meaningfully between the three promotions?
  • Which promotion performed better or worse than the others, and by how much?
  • Could differences in market characteristics explain the result instead?
  • Are the findings robust to outliers and to repeated testing?

The last two questions matter as much as the first. A ranking that disappears once market size is accounted for, or one that only holds in a single segment, is not something a company should reorganize a launch around.

04

Methodology

The reasoning behind each step, not just the sequence

Most of the work in this project was deciding what to do — the tests themselves were the short part.

01

Validate the experiment before testing anything

A clean p-value computed on broken assignment is worthless. Duplicates, stores drifting between promotions, missing weeks and assignment balance were all checked first, so that later results could be attributed to the promotion rather than to a data defect.

02

Match the analysis unit to the assignment unit

Promotions were assigned per store, but the file stores each location as four weekly rows. Treating those as independent would inflate the sample fourfold. Each store was collapsed to its four-week average, because the design earns 137 independent observations, not 548.

03

Check assumptions, then choose the test accordingly

Normality failed while equal variance held. Rather than force one method, ANOVA and a rank-based alternative were run together, so the conclusion had to survive both a parametric and a non-parametric route to count.

04

Follow the group test with a pairwise comparison

ANOVA can only say that groups differ, never which one wins. Tukey's HSD identified the specific pairs and reported the gap in dollars, which is the form the business actually needs.

05

Report effect size alongside significance

Significant and important are different claims. Effect size was reported to prevent a real but modest effect from being sold as a decisive one.

06

Try to break the finding before believing it

Three separate attempts: control for market size and store age, re-run without the one questionable observation, and correct for the false positives that repeated segment testing manufactures. A result that survives deliberate attempts to dismantle it is worth acting on.

07

Translate the statistics into money and a decision

The revenue gap was scaled to the affected stores and weeks, with its uncertainty stated. A recommendation without a magnitude is not actionable, and a magnitude without a range is misleading.

05

Analysis

Where the judgement calls actually happened

Was the experiment sound?

Nothing in the structural checks was broken — no duplicate rows, no store split across promotions, no missing weeks, and every store observed for the full four weeks. Assignment balance was also consistent with random allocation, so the three groups can be compared as designed.

548 rows in, 548 rows out. Nothing was deleted, because nothing was wrong. The outliers the IQR rule flagged were held over for a separate decision rather than quietly dropped here — removing rows silently is how a clean-looking result gets manufactured.

Why the data was reduced from 548 rows to 137

A store's week 1 and week 2 are not independent observations; they resemble each other. Aggregating to store averages fixes that, but it throws away the weekly detail, so the decision needed support.

Week carried no signal

Sales did not differ systematically across the four weeks, so averaging destroys no trend.

Variation lives between stores

Store-to-store spread was roughly 3.4× the week-to-week spread. Weekly movement was noise.

The complex alternative bought nothing

A mixed model would preserve weekly structure — but that structure was noise, so the added complexity had no payoff.

Choosing the statistical test

Normality not met

Driven by a handful of high-revenue stores in one large market, rather than by a fundamentally different distribution.

Equal variance met

Group variances were statistically similar, which keeps the parametric route viable.

Relying on ANOVA alone would have been hard to defend; relying only on a rank-based test would have cost the ability to state the gap in dollars. With balanced group sizes and equal variances, both were run and required to agree — and they did.

Which promotions actually differ

P2 · P3 Promotion 3 earns about $8,035 more per store-week than Promotion 2 real difference

Trying to break the finding

Three deliberate attempts to explain away Promotion 2's weakness, none of which succeeded.

Could market size be the real cause?

Market size turned out to be a far stronger driver of sales than promotion — but it was unrelated to how promotions were assigned, and store age was balanced across groups too. Controlling for market size actually made the promotion effect clearer, not weaker: a genuine confounder would have absorbed the effect, whereas removing an unrelated source of variation sharpened it.

Was one bad observation driving it?

Of the flagged outliers, nearly all came from a single large market — high sales because the market is large, not because the data is wrong. Deleting them would have deleted signal. One store had a single genuinely odd week; with no evidence of a data-entry error it was kept, and the analysis was re-run without it as a check. The conclusion did not move.

Does it hold in every segment — or did repeated testing manufacture it?

Promotion 2 was the weakest arm in small, medium and large markets alike. But testing each segment separately means running three tests, which inflates the chance of a false positive, so both Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg corrections were applied. All three segments stayed significant afterwards. The one caveat: the small-market segment holds only about five stores per arm, so it carries less weight than the other two.

06

Results

What the analysis established

Promotion 2 loses to both alternatives. Promotions 1 and 3 cannot be separated on sales.

Promotion 2 underperformed confirmed

Lower sales than both alternatives, holding across every market size and unchanged when the questionable observation is removed.

Promotions 1 and 3 are tied unresolved

No detectable gap overall, and splitting by market size flipped the direction rather than clarifying it.

How large is the effect?

Promotion explains roughly 8% of the variation in store sales — a medium effect by conventional standards. The remaining variation belongs mostly to market size and other store characteristics.

That number is worth stating plainly rather than burying. The promotion choice is a real lever, but it is not the dominant one, and a business planning around this result should size its expectations accordingly.

How to read these figures

The per-store, per-week gap is about $10,770 — modest until it compounds across 47 stores and four weeks. Stated the other way: $2M is not what one restaurant lost. Without naming the scale, the number reads as inflation.

It is also a point estimate. Carrying the confidence interval through the same arithmetic puts the total somewhere between $0.55M and $3.5M. Two million is the most plausible value in that range, not a settled amount — and since the outcome measured is revenue, the cost of running each promotion is not included.

Recommendation

Do not continue  Promotion 2

It underperformed on every check that was run: a real and non-trivial gap against both alternatives, consistency across all market segments, no confounding by market size or store age, stability when the questionable observation is removed, and a revenue impact large enough to matter commercially.

Undecided  Promotion 1 or Promotion 3

Sales data cannot separate them, so the choice has to be made on information this dataset does not contain — implementation and marketing cost, operational complexity, inventory and logistics burden, customer retention, and longer-term sales behaviour.

Naming what is still missing is more useful than forcing a winner out of a tie. It tells the business exactly what to measure before committing.

08

Limitations

What this analysis cannot answer

A four-week window

Long-term effects cannot be evaluated, and a durable effect cannot be told apart from initial novelty. Whether the ranking survives once the promotion stops being new is unknown.

Weekly detail was aggregated away

Averaging to the store level secured independence but removed any view of week-to-week dynamics.

No cost or profit data

The dataset carries sales but not promotion, labour, marketing or operational cost. This evaluates revenue performance, not profitability.

A thin segment

The small-market group contributes only about five stores per arm. The result held after correction but deserves re-testing with more data.

Future improvements

  • Mixed-effects models to use the weekly observations directly instead of averaging them
  • A longer observation window to separate durable effects from novelty
  • Cost and margin data, so the comparison can be made on profit rather than revenue
  • Customer-level behaviour and retention metrics
  • Interaction effects between promotion and market characteristics on a larger sample
