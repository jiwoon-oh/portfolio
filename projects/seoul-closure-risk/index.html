Executive summary

The whole project in one screen

Seoul allocates small-business support using a metric that ranks district size, not business risk. The category it flags first every quarter turns out to be the safer one.

The headline that started it. Korean restaurants top Seoul's net-decline ranking every quarter — 25.3% against 22.9% for the other nine food categories. That gap decides who receives consulting and emergency loans.

Two tests dismantled it

TestResultWhat it means
Permutation test · 1,000 shufflesp = 0.34488% of the +2.4 pp gap is the act of picking the highest of ten categories
Propensity matching · 7,042 pairs−5.77 ppOnce cells are genuinely comparable, the sign reverses
Woolf homogeneity testp < .0001…but that average is a blend, not a summary — it changes sign across size bands

Key findings

Store count alone reproduces the entire reversal. Foot traffic contributes nothing — it raises openings and closures alike, so it cancels in the subtraction. A busy district is not a safe one; it is a fast-turnover one.

The indicator decomposes exactly. Net decline = an event occurred × that event netting negative. Store count drives the first term and barely touches the second — so the metric measures how often something happens, not how badly it goes.

Seoul's own contraction indicator scores lift 0.937 — worse than random at predicting the following quarter. Both tools currently in use are broken, in different ways.

The model earns its place at the operating point, not on the leaderboard. Top-50 precision 0.760 against 0.380 for a store-count rule — and the two lists overlap by only 6%, so 47 of the 50 flagged districts are ones the current process never reaches.

Two results were measured and thrown out. One of them was a headline number this project had produced itself: a correlation of 0.515 that turned out to exceed what the target could support.

Recommendation

Rank cells, not categories

Stop using net decline as a standalone measure of vulnerability. Publish closures per store beside it, and allocate at the level of one neighborhood commercial district × one food category — not by industry rank.

01

Overview

What was audited, and why it was worth auditing

Seoul's Commercial District Analysis Service publishes a net decline flag for every commercial district × food category, every quarter — true when closures outnumber openings. District officers rank food categories by that flag and send consulting and emergency loans to the top of the list.

Nobody had checked whether the ranking means what it appears to mean. This project is that check: 13 quarters of Seoul commercial-district data, 67,475 cells, ten food categories, run through causal inference and forecasting to ask whether the metric identifies risk or merely identifies size.

The unit of analysis

A cell — one commercial district × one food category × one quarter. Everything on this page is measured at that unit unless stated otherwise.

Two similar words

Commercial district is a micro trade area of a few blocks (~2,800 of them). City district is one of Seoul's 25 boroughs. They are not interchangeable, and the difference matters for the recommendation.

The answer changes an action. If the gap comes from size confounding, category-level allocation has to be replaced by cell-level allocation conditioned on size. If it does not, the current process stands.

02

Business question

Who decides what with this number

Korean restaurants sit at #1 on the net-decline ranking every quarter. They are also the largest category in neighborhood districts, averaging 21.3 stores per cell against 10.3 for the other nine. More stores means more open/close events, and more events means more chances to land on the wrong side of a subtraction.

Main question

Does controlling for commercial scale and foot traffic change the apparent difference in net-decline risk between Korean restaurants and other food categories?

Stated as a hypothesis before the analysis: matching on those two variables will narrow the gap.

Sub-question

Among sales, foot traffic and store density, which commercial characteristics actually relate to next-quarter net decline?

This one turned out to have a more interesting answer than expected — one of the two named variables contributes nothing at all.

A five-store floor −54.1% of rows

The reason is the label, not the sales data. A three-store cell swings to −33% net decline when one shop closes. That is sample size, not district decline — and left in, those rows teach any model to find small cells rather than risky ones.

Sales missingness points the same way: 45.4% overall, 7.1% at five stores or more, 100% at two or fewer. A rate of exactly 100% is a rule rather than attrition, but what that rule is could not be confirmed — no published suppression threshold was located. It is reported as an observation, not a cause.

Note the filter is on store count, a covariate — not on missingness. The label is observed for every cell either way, so this narrows the population rather than selecting on the outcome. The cost is 54.1% of rows, which is why every conclusion carries the scope note “cells with five or more stores.”

A column that changed meaning would have biased every category comparison

In the 2023–24 files, store_count is not total stores — it is non-franchise stores. The 2025 schema renamed it and introduced a separate total. Two identities confirm it at a match rate of 1.0000.

Correcting the mapping moved the sample from 55,795 to 67,475 (+20.9%), and the increase was wildly uneven: chicken +390% against Korean +2%. Left uncorrected, franchise-heavy categories would have been systematically undercounted in exactly the comparison this project is about.

Sales excluded from the matching covariates

Missingness differs by category, and category is the treatment variable. Including sales would drop control cells selectively and bias the match itself.

A three-layer guard on every join

A duplicated key raises no error — it silently multiplies rows. Key uniqueness was asserted before merging, the merge was run with a declared relation, and the row count was checked after. All three fired clean on every merge.

04

Methodology

How the question was turned into something testable — and how each answer was checked

The pipeline

#StepDecision that mattered
1Define the labelNet decline in quarter t+1, built inside each (district, category) group so no cell borrows another's future
2Let the distribution pick the testNormality failed in all 39 group comparisons, so Kruskal-Wallis was used rather than ANOVA — chosen from the data, not from preference
3Test whether the gap is realPermutation test, because the treated category was selected as the maximum of ten
4Make the groups comparable1:1 propensity matching inside a caliper, exact-matched on quarter, without replacement
5Attack the resultDoubly robust estimation, counterfactual simulation, an alternative outcome label, and a split by size
6Predict, then benchmark honestlyRolling validation against a baseline ladder and against the city's own indicator

Why matching rather than regression adjustment

The overlap problem is the point of the exercise. The two groups differed by a full standard deviation in store count. A regression would report a coefficient by extrapolating into the region where no comparable control cell exists. Matching cannot do that — a treated cell with no partner is dropped and counted, so the failure becomes a visible number rather than a silent extrapolation.

Regression was not discarded: it runs as the doubly robust estimator alongside the match, which is why both numbers are reported.

Why gradient boosting margin within noise

Six candidates were compared. The gap between rule-based baselines and any model is large; the gap between the models is not — across six rolling quarters, boosting beats logistic regression by 0.002 AUC, while the quarter-to-quarter swing is five times that.

The deciding reason was not the score. 7.1% of cells still carry no sales figure, and this model splits on missing values natively. The alternatives need imputation — and missingness varies by category, which is the treatment variable, so imputing would reinject the very bias the design exists to remove.

The strength of this project is not the difficulty of the methods. It is the number of times a result was doubted — five times, and twice the result was thrown out.

05

Analysis

Six findings, in the order they were established

A · The gap was never real

Shuffling the category label 1,000 times breaks any relationship with the outcome while holding every group size fixed. Under that null, the top category still sits above the overall mean — because with ten finite samples, one of them is always lucky. Measuring that bump is the whole test.

Observed gap

+1.70 pp

What chance produces

+1.49 pp

Permutation p

0.344

Explained by selection

88%

p = .344. Ranking first is not statistically distinguishable from chance. This strengthens rather than weakens what follows — there was never a phenomenon called “Korean restaurants are risky” to explain, and the matched estimate is immune to this bias anyway, since it is computed inside matched pairs.

B · Conditioning reverses it

Each Korean cell was paired 1:1 with the nearest comparable non-Korean cell, exact-matched on quarter. Balance is checked before the outcome is read, so the design can be judged on its own terms:

CovariateBeforeAfterVerdict
log store count0.9990.037balanced
log foot traffic−0.4470.022balanced
log area−0.4730.031balanced
log attraction facilities−0.4610.032balanced

All four clear the |SMD| < 0.1 threshold. Only then is the gap worth reading.

C · But the average is not a summary

A mean will happily blend two effects pointing in opposite directions. Before reporting −5.77, the matched pairs were split by size to see whether that number is a summary or a blend.

Why the sign flips

“Korean cells are bigger” cannot be the answer — matching already fixed store count, so the mean is identical on both sides of every pair in the top band. Two candidates were checked, and only the second holds up.

Korean restaurants carry a median of 16 stores per cell against 6–10 for every other category. Matching on the absolute count therefore pairs a cell that is small for a Korean cell with one that is large for its own category — and that mismatch inverts as the bands climb. The two series cross zero in the same band and track each other closely.

The reading is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: part of −5.77 pp is not “Korean is safer” but “a cell far below its own category's norm is safer than one sitting at or above its own.”

Stated as a hypothesis, not a result. Five bands give five points. Confirming it means re-matching on within-category percentile rather than absolute count — listed as follow-up work, not something this analysis settled.

The statement that survives: Korean restaurants are markedly safer in small cells, the advantage disappears past roughly twenty stores, and near forty it inverts. Quoting −5.77 pp on its own hides a band pointing the other way.

D · Only one thing moves the gap

Holding one covariate at a time at its matched value and re-predicting shows where the reversal actually comes from:

Held fixedPredicted gap
Nothing — as observed+3.07 pp
Store count only−5.74 pp
Foot traffic only+3.57 pp
Area only+3.20 pp
All four covariates−5.79 pp

Store count alone reproduces the entire reversal. Adding the other three changes it by 0.05 pp. In the regression the same pattern holds — store count dominates every other variable, while foot traffic is not significant.

Foot traffic being null is the interesting part, and there are two separate reasons for it.

Reason 1 · It cancels in the subtraction

Foot traffic raises openings and closures, and roughly 70% of the effect disappears in the difference. Busy districts are not dangerous — they are high-turnover, and net decline cannot see the distinction.

Reason 2 · It is a proxy for location

Within the same cell over time, foot traffic moves and opening/closure rates do not. The cross-sectional association was produced by time-invariant location traits — rent, access, district age — not by foot traffic itself.

E · What the metric actually measures

This is where the audit lands. Net decline is a strict subset of “an event occurred” — a cell with no openings and no closures cannot post a negative net. So the metric decomposes exactly, not approximately:

P(net decline) = P(an event occurs) × P(it nets negative | an event occurs)

43.7% of cells recorded a zero because nothing happened at all — not because nothing bad happened. A five-store cell has a 71% chance of no event in a quarter; a fifty-store cell has a 98% chance of one.

Estimating store count's effect at each stage separately: it is overwhelmingly concentrated in the first stage and essentially absent from the second. Once an event has occurred, size no longer predicts the direction. So the honest statement is not “bigger cells are safer” but “the indicator measures how often something happens, not how badly it goes.”

F · Not every category depends on where it is

Applying the test selected earlier — each category fixed, district types compared within it — the effect sizes span two orders of magnitude:

CategoryEffect size (ε²)Reading
Pub / bar0.1202Highly location-dependent
Cafe / beverage0.0652Location-dependent

…six categories between0.048 – 0.016Weak to negligible

The spread between the two ends is 600×. Chicken shops carry the same risk wherever they sit, which means district-level intervention cannot move them — a plausible read is high delivery share reducing location dependence, though nothing in this dataset measures delivery.

The interval comes from a city-district-level cluster bootstrap — cells inside one borough are not independent, and resampling them individually would fake a narrower interval. Across ten designs varying caliper width, replacement and seed, the estimate stays inside [−6.0, −4.0].

What −5.77 pp is an estimate of

An ATT, not an ATE: the effect on the treated cells that actually exist, which is the right question because the decision it informs concerns the cells currently topping the ranking. It is also restricted to the region of common support — 70.7% of treated cells were matched, and the unmatched 29.3%, mostly very large Korean cells, are excluded rather than extrapolated over.

What the model is actually worth

Aggregate ranking ability is roughly what store count alone delivers. That reads as failure until you look at where the flags actually go.

The single-quarter validation figure is slightly higher, but the model was selected on that quarter, so it is optimistic by construction. The rolling number is the one reported — and that the two agree to 0.002 is itself evidence the optimism is small.

07

Business insights

What the numbers mean for the people using them — and what to do about it

① The published metric ranks size, not risk

The definition itself — closures minus openings — promotes cells where events are frequent. Korean restaurants top it because their cells are large, not because they are fragile. The city's other instrument, the change indicator, does not work either. Both current decision tools are broken, in different ways.

② “Open where the foot traffic is” is half of a sentence

Foot traffic raises openings and closures alike. A busy district is not a safe one — it is a fast-turnover one. And within a cell over time it predicts nothing at all, so it is standing in for fixed location traits rather than acting on its own.

③ Neither “Korean is safe” nor “Korean is risky” is a true sentence

Conditioned on circumstances, Korean restaurants are the safer side below twenty stores — and the safer side by a wide margin. Above forty it inverts. The signal lives in the category-by-size combination, which is precisely what a category ranking cannot express.

④ The finest unit this data can support is the cell

Ranked at city district × category, the answer key reshuffles when you re-measure it — the ranking is not a stable property of the world at that unit. Cell-level scores are stored so the aggregation is possible, but publishing that ranking would be publishing noise. Declining to answer is part of the answer.

⑤ The model earns its place on small districts, not on the leaderboard

On aggregate ranking it adds little over store count. Hold size constant and store count stops discriminating entirely while the model keeps working — and small districts are exactly where early warning matters. Reading the aggregate number alone would lead to scrapping the model for the wrong reason.

Recommendations

01 Allocate by cell risk score, not by category rank

Who
District small-business offices allocating consulting and emergency loans
What
Replace the category net-decline ranking with the top-K cell risk scores. Set K from the caseload an officer can actually contact — precision falls as K grows.
Expected effect
Of 50 cells contacted, 38 are cells that actually decline against 19 under the store-count rule. And because the lists overlap by only 6%, 47 of those 50 are districts the current process never reaches.
Caveat
This means the micro commercial district, not the city district. City district × category rankings were tested and are not reportable.
How to verify
Two quarters later, compare supported cells against unsupported cells with similar risk scores — a natural control group

02 Stop publishing net decline on its own

Who
Commercial District Analysis Service, publication team
What
Show closures per store beside the net-decline rate. The two rank categories at −0.406 correlation, so either one alone yields the opposite conclusion. Also stop presenting the HL contraction flag as a risk signal.
Expected effect
Prevents the misreading the data itself produces — Korean restaurants currently rank #1 on net decline and #9 on closure rate, and the published figure only supports the first
How to verify
Ask dashboard users which categories are risky, before and after; or track how the figures get quoted in press coverage

03 Rewrite the foot-traffic line in startup counselling

Who
District small-business centre counsellors; credit guarantee foundation consultants
What
Replace “choose a district with heavy foot traffic” with “heavy foot traffic raises both your revenue ceiling and your closure risk — it means fast turnover, so carry more working capital.”
Expected effect
Fewer under-capitalised openings in high-footfall districts. Current guidance states half of the effect — foot traffic raises openings and closures both, and only the first is being mentioned.
How to verify
12-month survival rate of counselled openings, before and after the revision

04 Match the instrument to the category's location sensitivity

Who
Programme designers choosing between district-level and business-level instruments
What
Location-sensitive categories (pub/bar, cafe, Korean) get location diagnostics and district consulting. Location-indifferent categories (chicken, fast food) get cost-structure measures instead — delivery commissions, franchise terms, operating efficiency.
Expected effect
Stops spending district-level budget on categories it provably cannot move. The gap in location sensitivity between the two ends is 600×.
How to verify
Compare outcomes of district-level support for high- and low-sensitivity categories over four quarters

08

Limitations

What this cannot support

Aggregated quarterly counts cannot say which shop closed, and matching only balances what was measured.

The average is not a summary

The effect runs from −13.0 pp to +2.4 pp across size bands and changes sign (Woolf p < .0001). Quoting −5.77 pp alone conceals a band pointing the other way — the stratum table has to travel with it.

Scope

Neighborhood commercial districts, cells with five or more stores, ten food categories. Developed districts and traditional markets need refitting. Treated match rate 70.7% — very large Korean cells fall outside.

Unobserved confounding

Matching balances measured covariates. Rent, owner age, tenure and capitalisation are absent, so the results are adjusted associations rather than proven causal effects.

The unit of observation

Data arrives pre-aggregated to district × category × quarter, so which shop closed is unknowable. Most of the variation in closure counts is individual circumstance, and none of it is here.

Sales missingness is undiagnosed

The handling is conservative — excluded from matching, never imputed — but four checks were skipped, including whether the residual missingness correlates with the outcome and what the 100% suppression rule actually is. The ATT does not use sales, so the exposure is confined to the prediction model.

An undocumented covariate

The area field has no definition in the source. The values suggest full polygon area rather than commercial floorspace — an inference, not a fact. Fixing it alone barely moves the gap, so the conclusion does not depend on it.

No sealed test quarter

The validation quarter was also used to choose the model. Six-quarter rolling validation stands in and the two figures agree to 0.002 — but a genuinely sealed quarter would be better practice.

Known non-determinism

Nearest-neighbour matching is greedy and order-dependent. Re-running holds the pair count at 7,042 but changes which pairs are selected, moving the estimate in the second decimal. Conclusions are unaffected; pinning the sort order would make it bit-reproducible.
